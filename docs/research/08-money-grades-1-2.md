# Money for grades 1 and 2: research brief

Compiled 2026-10-05 for two planned hub games for ages 6 to 8 (US grades 1 and 2): **Market Stall** (`market-stall`) and **Coin Count Vault** (`coin-vault`). It picks up where [07-money.md](07-money.md) and the two shipped money games stop. Numbers in square brackets point to the source list in section 7. "Judgment" marks a call with no source behind it. "Secondary" marks a claim I read only through another paper's summary. Section 5 is not legal advice.

## Summary

- **What children can do:** at 6, most children name the coins and count pennies, nickels and dimes when taught, but counting a mixed pile is still hard: after instruction, 7 of 35 first graders counted a mixed set correctly [2]. By the second half of grade 2, in a small group given extra teaching, counting mixed coins was mostly in place (75% on four items), counting quarters lagged (67%), and 87% knew how many of each coin make a dollar [2]. Giving change is the last skill to settle: in a Polish sample of 24 children aged 6 to 8, only 21% gave correct change [4].
- **How teachers sequence it:** group like coins, count on from the largest, skip count by 5, 10 and 25, make one amount several ways, then the fewest coins, then change from $1 by counting up [6, 7, 8, 10].
- **Notation:** the Common Core standard says only "using $ and ¢ symbols appropriately" [15]. Guidance documents read it as whole cents or whole dollars, with no decimal point until grade 4 [17, 18, 20]. Florida writes the same limit into its benchmarks [24]. Texas and Virginia teach the decimal point in grade 2 [22, 26].
- **Bills:** an original, stylized play bill that copies nothing from a real note is not an illustration of US currency. The 18 U.S.C. 504 and 31 CFR 411 conditions then do not apply, and no design within the limits in section 5 is risky. A design that copies any part of a real note would be risky, and a web game could not meet the illustration conditions anyway, so such designs are ruled out.
- **Two progressions:** Market Stall runs from "is this payment enough?" with pennies, nickels and dimes up to change from $1 and $20 and a two-item sale. Coin Vault runs from counting a sorted pile of pennies, nickels and dimes up to bill collections, several ways, fewest coins and reading $ against ¢.

## 1. What 6 to 8 year olds can do with mixed coins and bills

### Evidence

**Naming and value**
- **Names by age:** in an interview study of 80 children aged 2 to 8, a majority named the penny at 3, the dime at 5, and "the nickel, quarter, one-dollar bill and five-dollar bill" at 6 [1]. At 6 "there was still evidence that the quarter and nickel were being confused" [1].
- **Size before value:** in the same study, children's first idea of "more" was "its equivalence to the size of the coin rather than its value". Choosing by size "continued through the age of seven", falling steadily from 4 to 7. Relative value "emerged around the age of six", and by 8 all children chose by value [1].
- **Polish data (Kupisiewicz 2004, 700 children, secondary via [4]):** at 5 to 6, children think a bigger note or coin is worth more. At about 7 they "learn to recognize different nominal values" and pay an exact amount, but still cannot compare a few coins with one banknote. At 8, "a significant number of children still have problems ... with giving the right change". At 9 they change coins and notes efficiently.

**Counting collections (one study with first and second graders [2])**
- After instruction, first graders named the coins nearly perfectly and matched values less well.
- First graders were accurate skip counting pennies (one-to-one) but "had more difficulty when using many-to-one correspondence as required with nickels, dimes, and quarters".
- Only 7 of 35 first graders counted a set of mixed coins correctly, whatever the teaching method. 35% knew that 100 pennies make a dollar, and 34% put the coins in order of value. The authors conclude: "First-grade teachers should not expect mastery of money concepts."
- Second graders in the spring (17 children referred because they had not yet mastered money after class teaching, then given small-group teaching): 100% skip counted dimes, nickels and pennies, 67% skip counted quarters, 75% counted mixed coins on four items, 87% knew how many of each coin make a dollar, and all ordered the coins by value.
- The samples are small (29 first graders analysed in the taught group, 17 second graders) and not randomly assigned.

**Dollars and change**
- An earlier study by the same authors with three second-grade classes found that "second-grade students can understand dollars", and that the taught and untaught groups differed most on half-dollars and "the ability to make change from one dollar" [3].
- Its literature review reports that children "do poorly on standardized tests when word problems, or math stories, involve money, especially when they are required to make change" (Pettit 1986, secondary via [3]).
- In 24 Warsaw children aged 6 to 8, 75% swapped a 5 zł coin for smaller coins and 71% counted out an exact price, but only 21% gave correct change from a 10 zł note for 7 zł 21 gr. Younger children (6 and 7) often did not understand giving change at all (41% of them), and the 8-year-olds understood it but miscalculated [4].
- On the pretest, 6 children called the penny's worth "nothing", and others gave a coin's worth as what it buys ("a sucker", "some gum") [2].

**What school asks for (section 4 has the quotes)**
- Grade 1: coin values with ¢ (Texas, Florida, New York), counting pennies, nickels and dimes by 2s, 5s and 10s (Texas, Virginia like coins only), and in Florida the value of $1, $5 and $10 bills up to $100.
- Grade 2: mixed collections with quarters up to $1, dollar bills, word problems with $ and ¢ (Common Core, Florida, New York, North Carolina, Texas, Virginia). The decimal point appears in grade 2 only in Texas and Virginia.

**For the games (judgment):** a strong 6-year-old who finished Ride Fare can count pennies, nickels and dimes to 99¢ and judge whether a payment is enough. Quarters in counting, $1 = 100¢, change from a quarter or a dollar, and bills above $10 belong to age 7 to 8 and sit in the upper half of each progression. ## 2. How teachers sequence it

**Counting a collection: sort, then count on from the largest**
- Illustrative Mathematics (IM) grade 2 starts money with like coins, counted "by counting on or skip counting by five or ten" [6]. For mixed sets, students "find the value of a mixed set of coins by counting on, adding, or grouping like coins and then adding their total values" [6].
- The next lesson adds the quarter, and teachers watch for students who "count on from the coin with the largest value" and "group like coins and add the value of each group" [7].
- Eureka Math grade 2 (Module 7, Topic B): "Beginning with the largest coin value (often the quarter), students count the total value of a group of coins ... skip-counting by fives and tens when there are multiple nickels or dimes" [10]. Its lesson 6 has two parts: "Count mixed groups of coins starting with the largest value coin" and "Count mixed groups of coins by making ten" [10].
- For bills, "students arrange bills from greatest to least, count on to find the total" [10].
- Proportional models (a paper dollar of 100 squares that coins cover by value) helped some second graders see that a group of coin pictures needs "counting on" [3]. This matches the hub's cups and dots (judgment).

**Skip counting by 5, 10 and 25**
- Texas grade 1: "count by twos, fives, and tens to determine the value of a collection of pennies, nickels, and/or dimes" [21]. No quarters in the count.
- Virginia grade 2: "Count by ones, fives, tens, and twenty-fives to determine the value of a collection of mixed coins and one-dollar bills whose total value is $2.00 or less" [26].
- IM opens the quarter lesson with a choral count by 5 so students see the pattern before counting coin sets [7].

**Making change by counting up from the price**
- Eureka lesson 11: "students learn how to make change from one dollar using counting on, simplifying strategies (e.g., the arrow way), and the relationship between addition and subtraction ... (e.g., $1 − 45¢ = ____ or 45¢ + ____ = 100¢)" [10].
- Eureka lesson 13 shows a student who "might bond the quarter with 5 pennies to make the next ten before counting on" [10]: count up to the next ten, then by tens.
- IM's "Shop with a Dollar" activity says students "need to think about $1 as 100¢" to find what is left [9].
- Ride Fare's step 8 already has the child hand back pennies from a dime for a 1 to 9¢ fare ([ride-fare.md](../games/ride-fare.md)). Counting up from the price is the same move with bigger jumps.

**Finding the fewest coins**
- Eureka lesson 10 adds "the added complexity of using the fewest number of coins (e.g., 67¢ equals 2 quarters, 1 dime, 1 nickel, and 2 pennies)" [10].
- IM lists "using quarters, then dimes, then pennies to use the fewest amount of coins" as a strategy to share [7].
- Taking the largest coin that fits, again and again (the greedy method), always gives the fewest coins for US coins: Kozen and Zaks note this "is the case for the system 1,5,10,25,50,100 of American coins" [13].
- For the set these games use (1, 5, 10, 25, 100, 500, 1000 and 2000 cents), I checked every amount up to 10,000¢ with a short script and found no amount where greedy uses more coins than the minimum. Kozen and Zaks prove that if a counterexample exists, the smallest one lies below the sum of the two largest coins (3,000¢ here), so the check is complete [13]. The script is in the task folder, not the repo.

**Making one amount several ways**
- Eureka lesson 9: "'Estella has 75¢ to buy a yo-yo. How many different ways could she pay for it?' Seventy-five cents might be recorded with 3 quarters or shown with 2 quarters, 2 dimes, and 5 pennies" [10].
- IM asks students to make 97 cents and to find coin combinations worth 100 cents [7, 8].
- North Carolina: "25 cents can look like a quarter, two dimes and a nickel, and it can look like 25 pennies, and still all remain 25 cents" [18].
- Virginia grade 2: "determine multiple ways to represent the value of a quarter using pennies, nickels, and/or dimes" and "Construct a set of coins and/or bills to total a given amount of money" [26].

**Reading and writing $ and ¢**
- **The standard itself:** 2.MD.C.8 reads "Solve word problems involving dollar bills, quarters, dimes, nickels, and pennies, using $ and ¢ symbols appropriately. Example: If you have 2 dimes and 3 pennies, how many cents do you have?" [15]. It says nothing about decimals and does not say to keep $ and ¢ apart.
- **Where the "keep them separate" reading comes from:** the Common Core has no decimals before grade 4, where 4.MD.A.2 adds money "including problems involving simple fractions or decimals" [16]. Achieve the Core's item notes for 2.MD.C.8 say: "At this grade, student work is strictly limited to whole numbers, since decimals will not be introduced until grade 4" [17].
- **North Carolina** splits the standard into "Quarters, dimes, nickels, and pennies within 99¢, using ¢ symbols appropriately" and "Whole dollar amounts, using the $ symbol appropriately", and explains: "Since students have not been introduced to decimals, problems focus on whole dollar amounts or cents" [18].
- **New York** drops $ from grade 2: "Students are not introduced to decimals, and therefore the dollar symbol, until Grade 4" [20].
- **IM** writes a dollar and a quarter as "$1 and 25¢" and tells teachers: "When you write dollar amounts you use the $ symbol. Use the ¢ symbol for the amount that cannot be grouped to make a dollar" [8].
- **Florida** writes it into the benchmarks: grade 2 problems involve "either dollar bills within $100 or coins within 100¢", and "the expectation is not to use decimal values" [24].
- **Texas and Virginia** teach the decimal point in grade 2: "use the cent symbol, dollar sign, and the decimal point to name the value of a collection of coins" [22], and "using the cent (¢) and dollar ($) symbols and decimal point (.)" for amounts up to $2.00 [26].
- **Bridges** (Math Learning Center) teaches that 40 cents "can be expressed as $0.40" as well as 40¢ [11].

## 3. Common misconceptions

**A bigger coin is worth more (dime vs nickel)**
- Children's first idea of "more" follows coin size; it fades from 4 to 7, and relative value appears around 6 [1].
- Kupisiewicz's 5 to 6 year olds linked size to value; at 7 they still could not compare a few coins with a banknote (secondary via [4]).
- A second-grade study's review: "young children are often confused by the U.S. monetary system, because relative coin sizes do not correlate with relative values" and some "believe ... that nickels are worth more than dimes, and that five pennies are more valuable than three dimes", which "begin to lessen at approximately ages seven or eight" (Furth 1978, secondary via [3]).
- After instruction only 34% of first graders ordered the coins by value [2].

**The number of coins stands in for value**
- "Many children find it difficult to distinguish between the value of a pile of coins and the number of coins in the pile" [5].
- IM uses this as a warm-up: three sets of 8 coins worth 8¢, 40¢ and 80¢, asking "Does that mean they all have the same value?" [9].
- Children under 6 chose 100 pennies over a $1 bill (Grunberg & Anthony 1980, secondary, as in [07](07-money.md)).

**Counting each coin as one**
- Bridges: "For some, the nickel will represent 1 because it is one thing." Children who have just learned to count by 5 make the opposite error: "it may be difficult to break from counting by 5's to counting by 1's" [12].
- First graders counted pennies one-to-one well and struggled with the many-to-one counting nickels, dimes and quarters need [2].

**Nickel and penny confusion**
- I found no study that measures nickel-penny confusion in particular at ages 6 to 8.
- The related errors with evidence: a nickel counted as one, like a penny [12]; the penny judged to be worth "nothing" [2]; and at 6 the nickel still confused with the quarter, the coin of the same colour [1].

**Notation errors**
- In a Bridges classroom, a team that won a dollar and 15 cents first read the number as "one hundred and fifteen", then proposed "a dollar sign before the 1 and a cent sign after the 5" before settling on $1.15 [11]. These are transcripts from a curriculum, not a measured error rate.
- I found no study measuring $.50, $0.50¢ or 50¢-versus-$50 errors in grades 1 and 2. They follow from the standards' split (¢ for cents, $ for whole dollars) and from Texas and Virginia adding the decimal point in grade 2 (judgment).
- For the games (judgment): show 50¢ and $50 side by side as different amounts with different material (coins vs bills), and never show a decimal point unless the owner opts in (section 6, open questions).

**Change errors**
- At 5 to 6, children see changing money as "exchanging money for something essentially similar" (Kupisiewicz, secondary via [4]).
- At 6 to 7 many children did not grasp giving change; at 8 they understood it but miscalculated; 21% of 24 children got the change right [4].
- Making change from one dollar was the largest gap between taught and untaught second graders [3].
- Likely error shapes, not measured in the sources found (judgment): handing back the price instead of the difference, counting up past the payment, and losing track when switching from ones to fives or tens [12].

## 5. US rules on illustrations of currency

Not legal advice: this is a reading of public statutes, regulations and agency pages, not a lawyer's opinion.

### What the rules say

**18 U.S.C. 504 [27]**
- Paragraph (1) permits "The printing, publishing, or importation ... of illustrations of" US postage and revenue stamps, "any other obligation or other security of the United States", and foreign notes and securities. 18 U.S.C. 8 defines "obligation or other security of the United States" to include "Federal Reserve notes" [48], so this covers paper money.
- The illustrations must meet three conditions:
  - "(i) all illustrations shall be in black and white, except that illustrations of postage stamps ... may be in color";
  - "(ii) all illustrations ... shall be of a size less than three-fourths or more than one and one-half, in linear dimension, of each part of any matter so illustrated";
  - "(iii) the negatives and plates used in making the illustrations shall be destroyed after their final use in accordance with this section."
- The same paragraph adds: "The Secretary of the Treasury shall prescribe regulations to permit color illustrations of such currency of the United States as the Secretary determines may be appropriate for such purposes." That regulation is 31 CFR 411.
- Section 504 itself does not require illustrations to be one-sided; that condition comes from 31 CFR 411.1 for colour illustrations.
- Paragraph (2), on electronic and digital images: "The provisions of this section shall not permit the reproduction of illustrations of obligations or other securities, by or through electronic methods used for the acquisition, recording, retrieval, transmission, or reproduction of any obligation or other security, unless such use is authorized by the Secretary of the Treasury."
- Paragraph (3), on film and video: permits "The making or importation of motion-picture films, microfilms, or slides, for projection upon a screen or for use in telecasting" of US and foreign obligations, with no colour or size conditions in its text, and adds "No prints or other reproductions shall be made from such films or slides, except for the purposes of paragraph (1), without the permission of the Secretary of the Treasury." Whether an interactive web game counts as film or telecasting is unclear; this brief does not rely on paragraph (3) (judgment).
- A 1992 amendment struck the old list of allowed purposes ("philatelic, numismatic, educational, historical, or newsworthy") [27]. The Supreme Court had already held that purpose test unconstitutional in 1984 while upholding the rest: "Section 504's size and color requirements are valid as reasonable manner regulations", and "the provisions of § 474 are of real concern only when § 504's requirements are not complied with" [36].

**18 U.S.C. 474 [28]**
- Makes it a crime, among other acts, when anyone "prints, photographs, or in any other manner makes or executes any engraving, photograph, print, or impression in the likeness of any such obligation or other security, or any part thereof", unless directed by a proper officer. This paragraph has no intent element.
- Another paragraph covers whoever, "with intent to defraud, makes, executes, acquires, scans, captures, records, receives, transmits, reproduces, sells, or has in such person's control, custody, or possession, an analog, digital, or electronic image of any obligation or other security of the United States".
- Subsection (b) defines "analog, digital, or electronic image" to include "any analog, digital, or electronic method used for the making, execution, acquisition, scanning, capturing, recording, retrieval, transmission, or reproduction of any obligation or security".
- Section 504's exceptions apply "notwithstanding any other provision of this chapter", which includes 474 [27, 36].

**31 CFR 411.1, colour illustrations [29]**
- Authority is given for "color illustrations of U.S. currency provided that:
  - (1) The illustration be of a size less than three-fourths or more than one and one-half, in linear dimension, of each part of any matter so illustrated;
  - (2) The illustration be one-sided; and
  - (3) All negatives, plates, positives, digitized storage medium, graphic files, magnetic medium, optical storage devices, and any other thing used in the making of the illustration that contain an image of the illustration or any part thereof shall be destroyed and/or deleted or erased after their final use in accordance with this section."
- The 1996 final rule dropped a proposed requirement to print "non-negotiable" across every colour illustration and dropped "immediately" from the destruction rule, to match 504's "final use" [30]. It also warns that "the criminal liability imposed by 18 U.S.C. 474 and other applicable sections ... could apply where a color illustration of U.S. currency fails to meet the requirements" [30].
- The 1995 proposal said the rule "recognizes technological advances in both computer graphics and other reprographics and requires that such methods comply" [31]. So digital colour images are covered by the same three conditions.

**Agency guidance**
- Secret Service: "The Counterfeit Detection Act of 1992, Public Law 102-550, in Section 411 of Title 31 of the Code of Federal Regulations, permits color illustrations of U.S. currency, provided" the size rule, "The illustration is one-sided" and the destruction rule are met; "Title 18, United States Code, Section 504 permits black and white reproductions of currency and other obligations, provided such reproductions meet the size requirement" [32].
- The Bureau of Engraving and Printing (BEP) and the Treasury's currency education site give the same three conditions [33, 34]. Both also quote 18 U.S.C. 475, which bars any "business or professional card, notice, placard, circular, handbill, or advertisement in the likeness or similitude of any obligation or security of the United States" [33, 34].
- The Treasury's currency podcast sums the size rule up as "your banknote image will appear significantly smaller or bigger than a real dollar bill" [35].
- None of these pages says how the size rule applies to an image on a screen, whose physical size depends on the display. I found no official guidance on that.

**Case law on "likeness" and "similitude"**
- The traditional test asks whether a fake bill "bear[s] such a likeness or resemblance to any of the genuine obligations ... as is calculated to deceive an honest, sensible and unsuspecting person of ordinary observation and care" [37].
- In *United States v. Harrod* (6th Cir. 1999), the court held that the part of 474 covering things made "in whole or in part ... after the similitude" of a note is broader: one-sided black-and-white photocopies of $5 notes, made and fed into change machines, were enough. "Nothing in § 474 requires that the fake bills be good enough to fool an honest and unsuspecting person." The court added that "Mere possession of poorly counterfeited bills, without more, would not be enough" because that paragraph requires intent to use [37].
- *Harrod* cites a First Circuit case (*Chodor*, 1973) "holding that bogus bills lacking serial numbers and a treasury seal sufficiently resembled real currency under § 474 to submit to the jury the counterfeit issue" [37].
- So leaving off serial numbers and seals alone does not settle the question. A design has to avoid resembling a genuine note at all.

**What a genuine note looks like (for the design limits)**
- Every current note measures 6.14 x 2.61 inches (about 156 x 66.3 mm) [44].
- The $1 has Washington's portrait with a black Federal Reserve seal on the left and a green Treasury seal on the right [44].
- Background colours: the $5 centre "is light purple, blending to gray near the edges" [45]; the $10 has "background colors of orange, yellow, and red" [46]; the $20 has "background colors of green and peach" [47].

### Plain answer for this project

**Are original, stylized play bills "illustrations of currency"?** In our reading, no.
- Sections 504 and 411 permit "illustrations of" US obligations, and 474 reaches impressions "in the likeness of any such obligation ... or any part thereof" [27, 28, 29]. Both are about pictures of genuine notes or of parts of them.
- A bill drawn from scratch is not a picture of any genuine note if it has: a code-drawn numeral, an invented animal or simple non-person silhouette instead of a president's portrait, no seals, no serial numbers, no signatures, no lettering, no lathe-work border, none of a real note's colours or layout, and non-genuine proportions.
- Classroom play money is sold and used throughout early-grades teaching (IM and Eureka both have students use play bills and coins [6, 10]), which fits this reading (judgment).

**If they were illustrations, could a web game comply?** No.
- A colour illustration must be one-sided, under 75% or over 150% of the real note in each linear dimension, and every "digitized storage medium, graphic files" used to make it must be destroyed "after their final use" [29].
- A published web game keeps its image files on the server and in every visitor's browser cache for as long as it is live, so the destruction condition cannot be met (judgment).
- Paragraph (2) of 504 also withholds the exception for images reproduced "by or through electronic methods" unless the Treasury authorizes it [27].
- On-screen size changes with the display (judgment; no agency page addresses it).
- So the game cannot rely on the illustration exceptions at all. It has to stay clearly outside "illustration" and "likeness".

**Design limits that keep the game clear of any doubt** (all judgment, built on the sources above)
1. **Original art only.** No photo, scan, trace or AI image of a real note as a source or reference input. The image model never sees a real note.
2. **No real-note elements**: no portrait of a real person, no Treasury or Federal Reserve seal, no serial numbers, no signatures, no "THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA" or any lettering, no fine lathe-work or guilloche borders, no security-thread or watermark look, no building vignettes. Section 474 reaches "any part thereof" [28].
3. **Not a real note's colours.** No green-and-black $1 scheme and no purple $5, orange $10 or green-and-peach $20 [44 to 47]. Use a toy palette, the same family across denominations, with colour never standing for value (as in Piggy Parade).
4. **Non-genuine proportions.** A real note is about 2.35:1 (156 x 66.3 mm) [44]. Use a squatter toy shape, about 2:1, with rounded corners, so no drawn bill is a scaled real note.
5. **Size.** On top of the shape change, keep every drawn bill well under 75% of a real note in both dimensions on the children's laptop: under about 117 x 50 mm. On a 15.6-inch 1366x768 panel at 100% scaling, one CSS pixel is about 0.25 mm, so a 240 x 120 CSS px bill is about 61 x 30 mm.
   - The hub's 96 CSS px minimum target sits far below this.
   - Cap bills at 280 x 140 CSS px (judgment). That stays under 75% of a real note on displays up to about 24 inches at 1080p (about 0.28 mm per pixel: 78 x 39 mm); a 32-inch 1080p screen would make a capped bill about 103 x 52 mm, a little over 75% of the height. Given points 1 to 4 this is not a legal line; it keeps every drawn bill visibly smaller than a real note on the laptop.
6. **One side only.** Bills never flip; there is no back side.
7. **Visibly a toy.** Thick soft outline, flat colour, a big code-drawn numeral (1, 5, 10, 20) in the bundled font, a simple friendly animal or shape silhouette in the middle. Nothing that could be printed and passed.
8. **Not used as advertising in note form.** No promotional image, card or sign shaped like a bill (18 U.S.C. 475 [33]). The hub has no ads; this matters only for future promotional art.
9. **Record it.** Each game's design doc records this finding and these limits, and the asset table records that bill art is original.

**Coins (briefly).**
- 18 U.S.C. 489 covers "any token, disk, or device in the likeness or similitude as to design, color, or the inscription thereon" of US coins, which are physical objects. A 1951 amendment removed language that could restrict coin pictures in publications [38].
- Secret Service guidance, as reprinted by a news site, says "Photographs, printed illustrations, motion picture film, or slides of United States and foreign coins may be used for any purpose" [43]. I could not find this sentence on a current secretservice.gov page, so it is cited from the reprint.
- 18 U.S.C. 491 covers physical tokens "similar in size and shape" used in machines [39].
- The Hobby Protection Act requires an "imitation numismatic item" made, imported or sold in commerce to be "plainly and permanently marked 'copy'" [40]. An imitation numismatic item is "an item which purports to be, but in fact is not, an original numismatic item or which is a reproduction, copy, or counterfeit" of one [41]. The FTC rule marks the item itself with the word COPY [42]. These are rules for physical replicas. A stylized coin drawn by code on a screen is not a manufactured item and does not purport to be a real coin (judgment).
- The existing Piggy Parade and Ride Fare coins already follow the 07 brief's rule: stylized, code-drawn or reviewed art, no inscriptions.

**Is any bill design in a game of this kind risky?**
- Within the limits above: no meaningful risk found. The owner's stop condition ("if the rules turn out to make any bill design risky") is not triggered for designs inside these limits.
- Outside them: a design that copies any part of a real note (a real portrait, seals, serials, lettering, the real colour scheme, or a scaled genuine layout) would be risky, because no web game can meet the illustration conditions. Such designs are ruled out.
- Exactly what is uncertain:
  1. No statute, regulation or agency page defines when a stylized drawing becomes an "illustration" or "likeness" of a note.
  2. *Harrod* shows courts can apply a looser resemblance test under part of 474, though that holding concerned photocopies of real notes made with intent to use them [37].
  3. No official guidance says how the size rule applies to on-screen images.
  4. Whether 504(3) (film and telecasting) could cover a game is untested.

  None of these affects an original design with no real-note elements, but they are the points a lawyer would look at if the owner ever wanted bills that look more real.

## 6. Proposed hidden learning progressions

### Shared rules for both games

- **Money rules (owner's brief):**
  - The child is the seller (Market Stall) or counts for someone else (Coin Vault: an animal's coins go into its vault for the day and leave with it).
  - No wallet, balance or till carries between rounds; the till and vault reset every round; no goal spans sessions.
  - Money never becomes a reward currency and never touches stars or stickers.
  - Every round ends with at least one star whatever happened.
- **Where Ride Fare stops** ([ride-fare.md](../games/ride-fare.md)): fares up to 99¢ with pennies, nickels and dimes, counted on by 10s, 5s and 1s; a swap stand; and handing back 1 to 9 pennies from a dime. Both new games start at that level and add quarters, bills, $ and the skills below.
- **Deliberate correct evidence** (same idea as Ride Fare and Piggy Parade):
  - A choice counts when the press that made it came at least 0.7 s after the child's previous press in play.
  - With keys, it counts when the child moved the highlight with an arrow key and waited at least 0.4 s before the action key.
  - Choices made during a demonstration, right after a hint pointed at the answer, or by mashing record nothing.
  - Each customer (Market Stall) or each vault task (Coin Vault) gives one result, right or not.
- **Moving between steps** (between rounds only; judgment, copied from Ride Fare so the hub stays consistent):
  - Move up one step when every counted result of the round at the current step was right and there were at least 3, or when 6 of the last 8 at the step were right.
  - Move down one step when 2 or fewer of the last 6 were right.
  - A step change resets that step's window.
  - Every round above step 1 opens with one warm-up item from the step below that records nothing.
- **Easing after misses:**
  - A miss is never wrong on screen: the customer or the vault keeper smiles, the coin hops back, and the task stays open until it is done.
  - After two misses on one task, the idle hint comes sooner and a ghost hand shows the next coin.
  - Misses only feed the move-down rule above, and only between rounds.
  - Every task can always be finished with what is on the counter or tray.
- **No stall:** a child who stays on a step keeps getting full rounds there. A keyboard-only child who never uses the arrows records no evidence; after 3 finished rounds at step 1 or 2 with no counted result, the step moves up by one, up to step 3, as in Ride Fare.
- **Representations:** every amount shows as cups or dots (rows of ten, split 5 and 5) with the numeral and its symbol beside them, drawn in code, so value never depends on coin size (sections 1 and 3).

### Market Stall: is the payment enough, and count up the change

Scope: one customer at a time picks an item with a price tag and puts a payment on the counter. The child decides whether it is enough and, when it is more, counts the change back up from the price. No "several ways" or "fewest coins" tasks; any correct change counts.

| Step | Age, grade | New idea | Material | Deliberate correct evidence | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6, grade 1 | Is it enough? | Prices 10 to 50¢ in ¢ with cups. Payments in pennies, nickels and dimes are exact or short by 1 to 10¢. The child either hands the item over or taps the customer's purse to ask for more. | First deliberate decision right: hand over when exact, ask when short | Ride Fare level coins; Berti & Bombi "money can be insufficient" ([07](07-money.md)); Texas 1.4(C) [21] |
| 2 | 6, grade 1 stretch | Payment can be more: count up the change | Prices that are not a multiple of 10 (for example 26¢); the payment is the next ten in dimes (30¢). The child hands back pennies; a counter climbs from the price (27, 28, 29, 30). | Change exact, no extra coin bounced | Ride Fare step 8; Eureka counting on [10] |
| 3 | 6 to 7, grade 1 (Florida) | Whole-dollar prices and bills | Prices $1 to $10 in $. Payments in $1, $5 and $10 bills: short, exact or more. Change in $1 bills counted up ($7, paid $10: 8, 9, 10). | Decision right; change exact | Florida MA.1.M.2.3 [24]; North Carolina whole dollars [18] |
| 4 | 7, grade 2 | Quarters in the payment | Prices up to 99¢. Payments include quarters; the child judges enough, counting from the largest coin. Change, when owed, at most 24¢, in pennies, nickels and dimes. | Decision right; change exact | Texas 2.5(A) [22]; IM and Eureka count from largest [7, 10] |
| 5 | 7, grade 2 | Count up through the next 5 and 10 | Change of 10 to 50¢ (price 23¢, paid 50¢: pennies to 25, then a nickel to 30, then dimes to 50). The counter shows each jump. | Change exact (any coins) | Eureka "make the next ten" [10] |
| 6 | 7 to 8, grade 2 | Change from $1 | The customer pays with a $1 bill, which pours 100 cups. Prices 5 to 95¢. The child counts up to 100¢ with any coins. | Change exact | Eureka lessons 11 and 12 [10]; IM "think about $1 as 100¢" [9] |
| 7 | 8, grade 2 | $20 and bigger jumps | Prices $1 to $19, paid with a $20 (or $10 and $5). Change counted up with $1, then $5 and $10 bills ($13: 14, 15, then 20). | Change exact | Florida bills within $100 [24]; Eureka bills [10] |
| 8 | 8, end of grade 2 stretch | Two items | A customer buys two items priced in ¢ (total up to 100¢) and pays with $1, or two whole-dollar items and pays with $20. Tags slide together and the cups combine before the change. | Change exact | 2.MD.C.8 and Florida two-step problems [15, 24]; Eureka lesson 13 [10]; IM "Shop with a Dollar" [9] |

- **Notes:**
  - Amounts never mix $ and ¢ in one tag; a payment is all coins or all bills, except the $1 bill at steps 6 and 8, which pours its 100¢ as cups before the change.
  - Step 1 needs nothing Ride Fare did not teach except the yes-or-more choice, so a strong 6-year-old can reach it.
  - At step 2 the counting is Ride Fare's step 8 with prices above 10¢.
  - The choice at step 1 is two-way and has no wrong answer on screen: handing over a short payment makes the customer notice and add a coin with a smile; asking for more on an exact payment makes the customer show an empty purse and the item goes. Each counts as not right for evidence only (judgment).

### Coin Count Vault: count collections, several ways, fewest coins, notation

Scope: an animal brings coins or bills to a small town vault. The child counts them, builds amounts, and labels them. No payments and no change. A collection is counted by tapping or dragging each coin or bill into the vault's counting tray, where it pours its cups; the child then picks the total from three amount tags. The tray shows cups only, never a running numeral, so picking the tag is the counting evidence (judgment).

| Step | Age, grade | New idea | Material | Deliberate correct evidence | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6, grade 1 | Count a sorted collection | Pennies, nickels and dimes up to 99¢, already lined up by kind with dimes first. Three ¢ tags, one right. | Right tag at first deliberate pick | Ride Fare step 6 level; Texas 1.4(C) [21]; Florida 1.M.2.3 [24] |
| 2 | 6 to 7, grade 1 | Sort, then count on from the largest | The same coins spill in a pile. The child drags them into rows by kind (largest row first), then counts. | Right tag (sorting not scored) | IM "group like coins", Eureka "starting with the largest value coin" [6, 7, 10] |
| 3 | 7, grade 2 | Count quarters by 25 | Collections with quarters, first quarters alone (25, 50, 75, 100), then with dimes, nickels and pennies, up to 100¢. | Right tag | Virginia 2.NS.4(b) [26]; Texas 2.5(A) [22]; IM quarter lesson [7] |
| 4 | 7, grade 2 | One amount, several ways | The vault has two locks that each want the same amount (up to 50¢). The first is filled for the child; the child fills the second with a different mix of coins. | Second lock exact and with a different coin mix | Eureka lesson 9 [10]; North Carolina [18]; Virginia 2.NS.4(a), (c) [26] |
| 5 | 7 to 8, grade 2 | Fewest coins | One lock with exactly as many slots as the fewest-coins answer (up to 99¢). Only the fewest set fills it; a coin that cannot lead to a full lock hops back. | Lock filled with no coin hopping back | Eureka lesson 10 [10]; IM [7]; greedy is optimal [13] |
| 6 | 8, grade 2 | 100¢ is $1 | Collections over 100¢. When 100 cups fill, they trade for a $1 bill, and the amount reads "$1 and 25¢" with no decimal point. | Right tag | IM lesson 17 [8]; Eureka lesson 11 [10] |
| 7 | 8, grade 2 | Count bills | $1, $5, $10 and $20 bills up to $100, sorted greatest to least and counted on. Tags in whole dollars. | Right tag | Eureka lesson 8 [10]; Florida bills within $100 [24]; North Carolina [18] |
| 8 | 8, end of grade 2 | $ against ¢ | Look-alike tags differ only by symbol (45¢ or $45; $1 and 5¢ or $15). The child picks the tag that matches a collection, then finishes a tag by choosing the $ or ¢ tile. | Right tag and right symbol | 2.MD.C.8 "$ and ¢ symbols appropriately" [15]; notation errors [11] |

- **Notes:**
  - Step 1 matches the top of Ride Fare (mixed pennies, nickels and dimes to 99¢) but asks for the total rather than a payment, so a strong 6-year-old can reach it.
  - At steps 4 and 5 the coins never run out, so every lock can be finished.
  - At step 5, a coin that would make the fewest-coins answer impossible hops back with a smile; the child is never stuck.
  - The decimal point ($0.45, $1.25) is not in either progression. It could be an extra step 9 in Coin Vault if the owner wants Texas and Virginia's grade 2 notation (open question below).

### Open questions for the owner

- **Decimal point:** leave it out (Common Core, Florida, New York, North Carolina) or add a top step for it (Texas, Virginia)? This brief leaves it out.
- **Bill denominations:** $1, $5, $10 and $20 as decided. Florida grade 1 stops at $10; $20 appears only from step 7 in both games.
- **Pennies:** the US stopped striking circulating pennies in November 2025 (see [07](07-money.md)). Both games keep the penny as the 1¢ unit, as the standards still do.
