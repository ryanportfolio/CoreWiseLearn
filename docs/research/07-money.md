# Money games for ages 4 to 6: research brief

Compiled 2026-10-04 for two new hub games using US dollars and cents. Extended to age 6 (US first grade) at the owner's request. "Judgment" marks a call with no study behind it. "Secondary" marks a source I read only through another paper's summary.

## Summary

- **What children can do:** at 4 and 5, children know money is for buying, but they treat coins as interchangeable tokens. Coin values and paying an exact amount arrive around age 7. So the core at 4 is telling coins apart. Value enters at 5 as a stretch, shown with visible dots, and becomes the main content at 6.
- **Standards:** no US standard asks for coin values before first grade. Common Core starts money in grade 2. Texas asks kindergartners to name coins and first graders to know their values. Colorado's preschool standards ask for sorting coins by color or size.
- **Images:** pictures of coins are legal to use. Color pictures of paper money are restricted. The hub's art rule already has counted items drawn in code, so draw stylized coins in code and skip bills.
- **Recommended pair:** **Piggy Parade** (what coins look like, no counting) and **Ride Fare** (what coins are worth; one coin can count as five). Neither has a shop.
- **Saving:** a strong third game, but it collides with "nothing is lost" unless both choices pay off.

## 1. Which money skills are realistic

### Evidence

**Development**
- **Stage sequence:** Berti and Bombi ran a longitudinal pretend-store study with 80 children ([Berti & Bombi 1981](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129504); stage list via [ERIC ED281649](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED281649.pdf)). The stages run:
  1. No idea of payment.
  2. Payment is required, but any money buys anything.
  3. Money types differ.
  4. Money can be insufficient.
  5. The exact amount must be given.
  6. Correct change.
- **Ages:** a 2016 review summarises Berti & Bombi 1988 and Kupisiewicz 2004 ([Trzcińska & Sekścińska 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4846666/)).
  - At 4 to 5, children "know that money is used in a shop but they still think that one coin buys one thing only".
  - At 5 to 6, they link a note's size to its value.
  - Around 7, they "learn to recognize different nominal values" and pay exact amounts, but still can't compare a few coins against a banknote.
- **Count over value:** children under 6 chose 100 pennies over a $1 bill even after being told the two were equal. This is Grunberg & Anthony 1980 (secondary), cited in [Scheinholtz, Holden & Kalish 2011](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4614-0475-0_3).
- **Common mistakes:** some young children think nickels are worth more than dimes, and five pennies more than three dimes. These ideas fade around 7 or 8 ([Tracy, ERIC ED427978](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED427978.pdf), secondary).
- **Relative value:** children start to judge the relative value of options at about 5 ([Benenson et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0122215)).
- **Illinois Early Learning Project** ([tip sheet](https://illinoisearlylearning.org/tipsheets/coins/)): "Most preschoolers do not yet understand the values and uses of money." Adults may explain that a nickel buys what 5 pennies buy, but "do not be surprised if the children don't fully understand this idea".

**Value versus count**
- "Many children find it difficult to distinguish between the value of a pile of coins and the number of coins in the pile" ([Bond, NRICH 2011](https://nrich.maths.org/articles/money-problems)).
- **Bond's fix:** pre-money tokens marked with 1, 2 or 5 dots, so the only way to read a token's worth is to count its dots. She pairs them with "fair swap" games.
- **Result:** after a few weeks with a Year 1 group (ages 5 to 6), all the low-attaining children in the group could value sets of 1p and 2p coins. This was a small classroom trial, not a controlled study.

**Financial-literacy guidance**
- **CFPB on ages 3 to 5:** executive function (planning, waiting, impulse control) is the main building block, so teach patience and waiting for a reward ([CFPB Building Blocks 2016](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/092016_cfpb_BuildingBlocksReport_ModelAndRecommendations_web.pdf)). Children this age are "usually too young to understand abstract financial concepts" ([CFPB milestones](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/money-as-you-grow/young-children/money-milestones/)).
- **Money as You Grow, ages 3 to 5** ([list](https://www.friendshipstatebank.com/money-grow)):
  - You need money to buy things.
  - You earn money by working.
  - You may have to wait to buy something you want.
  - Wants differ from needs.
- **CFPB coin lesson:** written for ages 5 to 7 (K to 1). It stresses that "a coin's size doesn't always mean it's worth more" ([guide](https://www.fhi360.org/wp-content/uploads/drupal/documents/cfpb_building_block_activities_learning-about-coins_guide.pdf)).
- **Whitebread & Bingham 2013** (secondary, via [Kid Wealth](https://kidwealth.com/money-habits-set-age-7/)): teaching young children explicit financial knowledge "is likely to be ineffectual in shaping or changing their behaviours".

**Saving**
- **Marble game:** children get 3 marbles, a small marble game to play now and a bigger one later.
  - Only 39% of 3 to 5 year olds saved one or more marbles, with no gain across ages. In a replication, 26% of 3 year olds did.
  - Saving rose when children were told they could save (1.6 vs 0.67 marbles).
  - It also rose when they split the marbles into two bowls beforehand.
  - It also rose when 4 year olds first acted out the future on a storyboard and imagined feeling happy about saving. Imagining the happy outcome worked better than imagining the sad one.
  - All of these are summarised in [Zhang, Vohs & Carlson 2024](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2024-jecp-imagining-future-saving.pdf), a study of 115 four year olds.
- **Play-economy board game** ([Sonuga-Barke & Webley 1993](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Children's-Saving:-A-Study-in-the-Development-of-Sonuga-Barke-Webley/6f9be33877d65f86324810d792ca16f636f87d78), via [Otto et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167487005000541)): banking tokens rose with age, with the largest jump between 6 and 9. Six year olds save because they think they ought to.

## 2. Pitfalls

**Coin confusion**
- **Real diameters:** dime 17.91 mm, penny 19.05 mm, nickel 21.21 mm, quarter 24.26 mm ([summary](https://www.thethinkacademy.com/blog/edubriefs-us-coins-for-kids-pennies-nickels-dimes-quarters/)). Penny and dime are close in size but differ in color. Nickel and quarter share a color, and the quarter is 14% wider.
- **Precedent:** Common Sense Media faulted Amazing Coin because its nickel and quarter look the same size ([review](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/amazing-coin)).
- **Keep true ratios:** with the dime at the 96 px floor, the penny is 102 px, the nickel 114 px and the quarter 130 px.
- **Use the other real cues:** copper vs silver, smooth edges on the penny and nickel, ridged edges on the dime and quarter, and a portrait silhouette.
- **Teach the heads side:** quarter backs change from series to series (judgment).

**Count beats value**
- Young children judge a pile by how many coins it holds (Grunberg & Anthony; NRICH).
- A machine that takes five pennies and returns one nickel can look like a loss to a 4 or 5 year old.
- Keep the five dots visible inside the nickel after a swap (judgment, from Bond's dot tokens).

**Abstract value with no voice**
- The game can't say "worth five cents".
- Show value as dots that pour out of a coin into a glass meter, with a numeral beside the dots. This follows the hub's existing numeral-beside-dots rule.

**Prices that need reading.** Show prices in three forms, in this order:
1. A price tag made of coin pictures, as in a [PreKinders](https://www.prekinders.com/preschool-money/) classroom store that labels items with penny stickers.
2. Dots.
3. A numeral with ¢ beside the dots, at age 6 only.

**Pennies are leaving circulation**
- The Mint struck its last circulating penny on 12 November 2025. Pennies remain legal tender ([US Mint](https://www.usmint.gov/news/press-releases/united-states-mint-hosts-historic-ceremonial-strike-for-final-production-of-the-circulating-one-cent-coin)).
- Rounding cash to 5¢: the House and Senate had each passed their own version, but neither was law as of late September 2026. Some states issued their own rules ([Governing](https://www.governing.com/finance/as-pennies-disappear-states-step-in-to-set-rounding-rules)).
- Standards still teach pennies, and the penny is the 1¢ unit. Keep it, but lean the progression toward nickels and dimes (judgment).

**Coin and bill images**
- **Coins are fine:**
  - A 1951 amendment removed the language in 18 U.S.C. 489 that could have restricted coin pictures in publications; the section now covers physical tokens ([Cornell LII notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/489)).
  - The Mint's design-use policy forbids implying Mint or government endorsement. It asks for the credit "United States Mint image" when you use the Mint's own pictures ([policy](https://www.usmint.gov/news/image-library/circulating-coin-design-use-policy)).
  - Some designs are copyrighted, such as the front of the Sacagawea dollar. The Mint also reserves rights in some contractor photos ([Wikimedia Commons guidance](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Determining_if_U.S._coins_are_free_to_use)).
  - Code-drawn stylized pennies, nickels, dimes and quarters raise none of these issues.
  - Leave off inscriptions: they are unreadable at this size and would count as text.
- **Bills, skip them:**
  - Color pictures of US paper money are legal only if they are under 3/4 or over 1.5 times real size in every dimension and one-sided. The files used to make them must also be destroyed after final use ([BEP, citing 18 U.S.C. 504 and 31 CFR 411.1](https://www.bep.gov/currency/currency-image-use/)).
  - On-screen size changes with the display, and a shipped game keeps its image files, so a game can't reliably meet these terms.
- **Hub rule:** the image model never draws counted items, so coins are drawn in code anyway.

**Commercial framing**
- Most children under 7 or 8 can't tell when an ad is trying to persuade them ([APA Task Force 2004](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/advertising-children)).
- Lures and pressure to buy in-app were common in apps used by 3 to 5 year olds ([Radesky et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)).
- Montessori Preschool uses coins as a reward currency for decorating a classroom ([CSM](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/montessori-preschool)). That is the pattern to avoid.
- **Rules for both games** (judgment, consistent with red line 6 in `docs/research/04-rewards-progression-ethics.md`):
  - No shop selling goods.
  - No wallet or balance carried between rounds.
  - Coins are never a reward currency. They are learning material and never touch stars or stickers.
  - Coins come from a tray that refills every round, so nothing is scarce and nothing is spent for good.
  - Coins buy an experience for someone else or for everyone (a ride, a show), never an item the child keeps.
  - No goal that spans sessions ("come back to fill your jar"); that would be a hook to make children return.

**Saving vs "nothing is lost."** In the marble studies, spent marbles are gone. In the hub, both choices must lead to fun, and the round still ends with at least one star.

- A PBS KIDS preschool coin game on pbskids.org. Curious George has only a printable.
- A Daniel Tiger savings episode. The closest is "Daniel Can't Get What He Wants", which is about wanting.

**Patterns to use**
- Show prices as coin pictures, then dots, then a numeral with ¢.
- Use a meter or jar that visibly fills. Children read a filling cup better than a progress bar (`docs/research/01-design-principles.md`).
- A wrong coin slides back to the tray while the right spot wiggles, with no error sound, as in Letter Train and Dino Picnic.

## 4. Candidate mechanics

**Shared rules for every candidate**
- **Motor difficulty, kept separate from learning:**
  - coin scale (the dime never under 96 px; the others at true ratio)
  - how many coins sit on the tray (2 to 6)
  - drop-zone size and snap distance
  - drag, or click a coin and then its target
  - keyboard highlight
- **Learning evidence:** only deliberate drops count, per the hub rule.
- **Learning steps:** they change only between rounds, adding one new idea at a time.
- **[6]:** marks steps that appear only in the age-6 part of the progression.

### A. Piggy Parade (sort coins by look)

- **Core play:** coins tumble from a fountain. Glass piggy banks each wear one coin on the belly. The child drops each coin into the matching piggy, which oinks, wiggles and stacks the coin inside.
- **Skill:** telling coins apart by color, size, edge and face; heads to tails; at 6, value order and ¢.
- **Progression:**
  - L1 (age 4): penny vs quarter, the most different pair.
  - L2: penny vs dime, close in size but a different color.
  - L3: penny, nickel and dime.
  - L4: all four coins.
  - L5 (age 5): the piggy shows tails and the coin shows heads.
  - L6 (ages 5 to 6): piggies stand on steps in order of value, each showing its value as dots grouped in fives. The child's coin lands on its step.
  - L7 **[6]**: piggies wear 1¢, 5¢, 10¢ and 25¢, with dots.
  - L8 **[6]**: the four coins line up by size, then by value, so the dime jumps ahead of the nickel and penny. This is the CFPB lesson shown as motion.
- **Demonstration:** the ghost cursor-character drops one coin. The matching belly coin glows and the piggy swallows it.
- **Not a shop:** nothing is bought. Coins are sorted, as in a classroom coin wash.
- **Main risk:** thin play, close to Letter Train's matching. It needs a strong physical feel (coins roll and spin, piggies fill up) and true coin sizes. Naming waits for voice clips.

### B. Ride Fare (pay the ride for animal friends)

- **Core play:**
  - Animals queue for a carousel or little train, and a sign over the fare box shows the fare.
  - The child drops coins in, and each coin pours its value as dots into a glass meter.
  - When the meter is exactly full, the animal climbs on and the ride turns.
  - An extra coin bounces back to the tray.
- **Skill:** coins have values; value is not the number of coins; exact payment; at 6, mixed coins up to 99¢.
- **Progression:**
  - L1 (age 4): the sign shows the exact coins, and the child drops in the same coins (1 to 3). No counting.
  - L2 (ages 4 to 5): pennies only, fares of 1 to 5 dots. Keep this step short; it overlaps Dino Picnic.
  - L3 (age 5): the nickel. The fare is 5 dots and the tray holds five pennies and one nickel. Both pay, and the nickel pours five dots at once.
  - L4 (age 5, stretch): fares of 6 to 10 dots, paid with a nickel and pennies.
  - L5 **[6]**: dimes; fares up to 20 dots, grouped in fives and tens.
  - L6 **[6]**: the fare shows as a numeral with ¢ beside the dots. Pennies, nickels and dimes up to 99¢, counting on by 10s, 5s and 1s.
  - L7 **[6]**: a swap stand at the stop. Five pennies become a nickel and two nickels a dime, and the dots stay visible inside.
  - L8 **[6, stretch]**: an animal pays a dime for a 7¢ fare, and the child hands back 3 pennies.
- **Demonstration:** show the goal first, a full meter and an animal riding. Then the ghost drops a nickel and five dots pour out.
- **Not a shop:** the child helps others ride. Nothing is bought or kept, the tray refills, and there is no balance.
- **Main risk:**
  - L1 and L2 repeat Dino Picnic.
  - "A nickel counts as five" may not hold at age 5, since coin values arrive around 7. The hidden difficulty must not wait on it.
  - The 4 year old must get a full game out of L1 to L3.

### C. Fair Swap Machine (exchange)

- **Core play:** a friendly machine has five slots. The child fills them with pennies and the machine presses one nickel, which keeps its five dots in a ring.
- **Skill:** equal value (5 pennies = 1 nickel, 2 nickels = 1 dime). **[6]** 5 nickels = 1 quarter.
- **Progression:** dot tokens (1, 2 and 5 dots), then pennies to a nickel, nickels to a dime, pennies to a dime, then **[6]** mixed coins and the quarter.
- **Main risk:** to a child who judges by count, five coins in and one coin out looks like a loss. It works better as step L7 inside game B than as a game of its own.

### D. Two Jars (later or now)

- **Core play:** coins appear on a short walk. Each goes either to a small fountain (an instant splash and a jumping fish) or to a big jar. The jar fills toward an end-of-round show: a hot-air balloon that grows with the jar.
- **Skill:** waiting for bigger fun and planning ahead. **[6]** Spend, save and share as named ideas, and counting what was saved.
- **Progression:**
  - L1: one jar only, so the child sees the jar lead to the show.
  - L2: two jars. First a demo shows the big show a full jar earns, the kind of happy-future preview that raised saving in Zhang et al.
  - L3: plan first. The child splits coins between the two before the walk, like the two-bowl planning that helped 3 to 6 year olds.
  - L4 **[6]**: the balloon has a fare in ¢ and the jar shows ¢.
  - L5 **[6]**: a third jar to share with a friend, who reacts with delight.
- **Not a shop:** the goal is a show, not an item, and everything resets each round.
- **Main risk:**
  - A smaller show can read as a loss, which breaks "nothing is lost".
  - Without words, 4 year olds may not see that the jar choice matters, and most preschoolers save little on their own.
  - A goal that carries over between sessions would be a hook to make children return, so keep it inside one round.

### E. Lemonade Stand (child is the seller)

- **Core play:** animals walk up and pay with coins, and the child pours one cup per payment.
- **Skill:** earning by work; checking a payment; **[6]** giving change.
- **Main risk:** it's framed as selling goods; the core at 4 to 5 is penny counting, which is Dino Picnic again; giving change belongs to ages 7 to 8.

### F. Coin Rubbing Studio (creative)

- **Core play:** rub a crayon over paper to reveal hidden coins, heads or tails, then stamp them into a picture saved to a gallery.
- **Skill:** coin faces and details.
- **Main risk:** little to progress through, and it repeats Shape Workshop's creative mode.

## Recommendation

Build **A. Piggy Parade** and **B. Ride Fare** as the pair.

- **They split the subject the way standards and studies do.** A is what coins look like: kindergarten and Colorado pre-K skills, with no counting. B is what coins are worth: first-grade skills. Each keeps one rule per round.
- **Neither repeats Dino Picnic.** A has no counting at all. B leaves one-coin-one-dot behind after its first two steps.
- **Both run from 4 to 6 without new screens.** The age-6 steps (¢, mixed coins up to 99¢, swaps, change within 10¢) sit at the top of the hidden learning progression.
- **The others:** C fits inside B as step L7. D is the best third game if the owner wants spend and save, but only if both choices end in fun inside one round.

## Open questions for the owner

- **Coin faces:** plain code-drawn silhouettes, or one reviewed AI-drawn relief per coin face? The current art rule bars the image model from drawing counted items.
- **Pennies:** keep them despite the 2025 phase-out? This brief says yes, but leans the progression toward nickels and dimes.
- **Ride Fare's setting:** carousel, little train or ferry. None involves goods, so pick by the children's interests.
- **Voice clips:** clips for penny, nickel, dime and quarter would make naming teachable. Until then, naming is out of scope.

## Gaps in the evidence

- **Not read directly:** I couldn't fetch the Kalish review or the Whitebread & Bingham report, and the Tracy paper is a scanned PDF I couldn't read. Grunberg & Anthony, Tracy, and Whitebread & Bingham are cited secondhand, as marked.
- **Not confirmed:** I found no Daniel Tiger savings episode, no PBS KIDS preschool coin game, and no Khan Academy Kids coin activity.
